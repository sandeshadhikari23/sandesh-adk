Who I am

An engineer who writes the code, and a programmer who has tested concrete by hand.

I studied civil engineering in Nepal, where earthquakes are not hypothetical. Today I work on the question that follows from that: with limited money and an uncertain future, where does protection matter most?

My answer is computational. I build simulations that play out thousands of possible futures, statistical models that learn to imitate slow physics in milliseconds, and optimizers that search through more options than anyone could list. I worked as a software developer before the Ph.D., so I build these tools end to end and I care whether they run fast.

Infrastructure is where I learned this. The shape of the problem is the same wherever capital meets risk: too many choices, incomplete information, and a real cost to being wrong. That is the work I want to keep doing.

Away from the keyboard: hiking, camping, running and the gym. I rarely turn down something I have not tried before.

Why the real thing is hard

01

The future is a distribution

Nobody knows which earthquake arrives. A plan has to hold up across thousands of possible ones, so it is judged on a whole range of outcomes and never on one forecast.

02

The options explode

The toy above has millions of ways to choose six links. A real network with 300 pipes has more possible plans than there are atoms in the observable universe.

03

Every answer is expensive

On a real system, scoring one plan means running physics: pressure in every pipe, or traffic on every road. A single run can take minutes to hours, and a search needs thousands.

Track 01 · How I attack it

Three moves, in this order.

  1. Simulate

    Play out the uncertainty. Thousands of possible events, each one damaging the system in a different way.

  2. Approximate

    Train a fast statistical model to stand in for the slow simulator, one that also knows where it is guessing.

  3. Optimize

    Search with the fast model in the loop, and spend real simulations only where they could change the decision.

Why not just train a machine learning model?

It is the first thing people suggest. For this problem it is the wrong tool on its own, for three reasons.

01

There is almost no data to learn from

Machine learning needs many examples. Here the earthquake has not happened yet, so every example has to be simulated, and one simulation can take hours. That buys a few hundred examples, far fewer than a large model needs.

02

It has to know when it is guessing

An optimizer hunts for unusual plans, which is exactly where a trained model has seen nothing. Most models answer confidently anyway. I need one that reports its own uncertainty, so I know where the next simulation is worth paying for.

03

A prediction is not a decision

Predicting the damage for one plan does not say which plan to choose out of millions. And a black box cannot be handed to an exact solver, or explained to the people spending public money. A simple model with a known form can do both.

Where plenty of real data exists, machine learning is the right tool. I have used it there, in co-authored work on predicting how long highway construction projects will take.
